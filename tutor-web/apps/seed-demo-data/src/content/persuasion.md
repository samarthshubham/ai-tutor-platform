# Writing to persuade

Persuasive writing gives readers a reason to consider a choice or change their minds. Start with a clear claim: tell the reader what you propose. Then give a reason that matters to the audience and support it with evidence. Evidence may be an observation, a reliable source, or a specific example. Explaining the connection between evidence and claim is part of the work. A reader may agree that the evidence is true and still wonder why it supports your proposal.

## Think about the audience

A principal considering a new school activity might want to know whether it helps students and how much staff time it requires. A group of students might care more about where it takes place and what they can do there. You can use the same central claim for both audiences while choosing evidence that answers their different questions. Try to imagine a reader who is genuinely undecided. What would they need to know before making a choice?

## Worked example: proposing a quiet lunch space

Suppose your class wants the library to offer a quiet lunch space one day each week. A claim could be: “Our school should try a weekly quiet lunch in the library for one month.” A reason could be that some students need a comfortable place to read or take a break. Imagine your class surveys forty students and sixteen say they would try the space. Sixteen divided by forty is 0.4, or 40 percent. In the proposal, write “Sixteen of the forty students we surveyed said they would try it.” The survey describes the people you asked, so avoid saying that 40 percent of the whole school will attend.

Now connect that evidence to the proposal. Interest in one class suggests a trial could be worthwhile. A short trial would let the school count the students who actually come and decide whether a longer program makes sense. The count and the trial serve different purposes: the survey checks interest, and the trial checks what happens when the option is available.

## Answering a reasonable objection

An objection could be that a teacher would need to supervise the library. Present it fairly, then give a practical response. A proposal might suggest starting on one day when the library already has staff present and checking with the librarian before choosing the date. If the library cannot provide supervision, the trial would need to change. Naming this condition makes the proposal more precise.

## Finish with a request

End by asking for a specific next action that fits your claim. In this example you could ask the principal to discuss a one-month trial with the librarian. Read your draft again and mark the claim, reasons, evidence, objection, and final request. Make sure each part helps the reader understand the choice you want them to consider.
