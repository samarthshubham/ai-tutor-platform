# How cells release energy

A cell uses energy when it moves substances across its membrane, builds molecules, or contracts. Glucose is one fuel that cells can break down. Cells transfer some of its chemical energy to a molecule called ATP. When the cell breaks down ATP, the released energy can drive activities that need it. The cell regularly makes more ATP because its supply is small compared with its daily energy needs.

## From glucose to usable energy

Cellular respiration begins with reactions in the watery part of the cell outside the nucleus. These reactions split glucose into smaller molecules. In animal and plant cells, when oxygen is available, mitochondria carry out most of the remaining steps that release energy from those smaller molecules. Carbon dioxide and water form, and cells capture part of the released energy in ATP. Energy also leaves as heat.

A summary of aerobic respiration is:

```text
1 glucose + 6 oxygen → 6 carbon dioxide + 6 water + released energy
```

The numbers count molecules in the balanced summary. They do not specify how many ATP molecules each cell makes. The oxygen that reaches a muscle cell comes from the air breathed into the lungs and is carried in the blood. The carbon dioxide formed by cells travels in the blood to the lungs, where it can be breathed out. Breathing and cellular respiration are therefore closely related processes that happen in different places.

## Cells with little oxygen

During very intense exercise, a muscle can need ATP more quickly than aerobic respiration alone can provide it. Some glucose can be broken down without using oxygen, with lactate forming in the muscle. This route provides much less ATP per glucose molecule than aerobic respiration. Yeast cells use a different route without oxygen that makes ethanol and carbon dioxide. The gas released by yeast can form bubbles in bread dough.

## Worked example: tracking atoms

Take two glucose molecules in the summary equation. Each glucose molecule contains six carbon atoms. Two glucose molecules contain twelve carbon atoms in all. Multiply the coefficients in the equation by two: twelve oxygen molecules are used, and twelve carbon dioxide molecules and twelve water molecules are formed. Each carbon dioxide molecule contains one carbon atom. All twelve carbon atoms of the original glucose are now accounted for in the twelve carbon dioxide molecules.

## Respiration in a plant

Plant cells carry out cellular respiration during the day and at night. A green leaf can also carry out photosynthesis in light, when the conditions are suitable. During the day a rapidly photosynthesizing leaf may take in more carbon dioxide than its cells release. When the light goes away, photosynthesis stops while cellular respiration continues. A measurement of gas exchange therefore tells you the combined effect of both processes under the conditions you tested.
