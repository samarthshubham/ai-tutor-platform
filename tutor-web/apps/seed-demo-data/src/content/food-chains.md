# Who eats whom

An organism needs energy to grow and carry out the activities that keep it alive. A food chain describes one path energy can take from one organism to another. In most ecosystems the path begins with a producer such as a plant or an alga. Producers use energy from sunlight to make sugars. Consumers obtain energy by eating plants, animals, or other organisms.

## Reading arrows

An arrow in a food chain points from the organism being eaten toward the organism that eats it. It shows the direction of energy transfer. In the chain grass → grasshopper → frog → snake, energy stored in the grass passes to the grasshopper when it feeds. Some energy stored in the grasshopper then passes to the frog. The grass is the producer, the grasshopper is a primary consumer, the frog is a secondary consumer, and the snake is a tertiary consumer in this chain.

The names refer to positions in a particular chain. An omnivore can eat food from more than one position. If a bird eats a seed, it is acting as a primary consumer. If the same bird eats a caterpillar that fed on a leaf, the bird is acting as a secondary consumer in that chain.

## Worked example: estimating a transfer

Imagine producers add 10,000 kilojoules of energy to new plant tissue in an area during a month. Suppose that 10 percent of that energy ends up in new tissue of the herbivores that eat those plants. The herbivores then store 10,000 × 0.10 = 1,000 kilojoules of new tissue energy. If predators store 10 percent of that amount, they store 1,000 × 0.10 = 100 kilojoules.

Ten percent is an estimate for this example. Real transfers differ among ecosystems. Organisms use energy for movement and other activities, and some energy leaves as heat. Some parts of organisms are never eaten and some eaten parts cannot be digested. These are reasons why only a fraction of the previous level's energy becomes new tissue at the next level.

## From chains to webs

Food chains join together into a food web. A frog might eat grasshoppers, beetles, and flies. A snake might eat frogs or mice. A drop in grasshopper numbers would reduce one food source for frogs. How much the frogs are affected depends on whether other insects are available. Drawing the connections helps you predict which organisms may be affected directly and which effects may take longer.

Bacteria and fungi often act as decomposers. They break down dead organisms and waste, returning nutrients to the environment where producers can use them again. Nutrients cycle through an ecosystem. New energy enters most ecosystems as sunlight and eventually leaves as heat.
