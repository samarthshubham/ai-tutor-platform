# Building a paragraph

A paragraph develops one main idea. It gives a reader a manageable part of a larger argument, explanation, or story. In a short explanatory paragraph, a good starting point is a topic sentence that names the subject and makes a specific point about it. The following sentences explain that point, give evidence, or offer an example. A final sentence can show what the evidence means or help the reader move to the next paragraph.

## Finding the main idea

Imagine you want to explain why a school garden can help students learn science. The sentence “The school garden has many plants” tells us what is there, but it gives the writer few clues about what to explain. “A school garden lets students observe changes in plants over time” makes a more focused claim. A reader can expect details about observation and change. Details about the school's sports teams or the price of a new fence belong elsewhere unless you connect them to that claim.

## Worked example: developing a point

Start with the focused topic sentence: “A school garden lets students observe changes in plants over time.” Add a concrete detail: “For three weeks, our class measured the height of bean plants every Monday.” Then explain what students did with those observations: “We plotted each week's measurements on a graph and compared growth in sunny and shaded parts of the garden.” Finish by connecting the example to the point: “The repeated measurements turned an everyday change into evidence the class could discuss.”

Notice the order. The reader first learns the point, then learns what happened, and finally sees the connection. The sentence about graphing supports the paragraph because it shows how students used observations to learn. A claim about sunlight causing faster growth would need more evidence, such as a fair comparison between similar plants. Good paragraphs say exactly as much as their evidence can support.

## Making sentences connect

A transition tells the reader how one sentence relates to the previous one. “For example” introduces a specific instance. “Because” gives a reason. “However” signals a contrast. Choose a transition only after working out the relationship between the ideas. Sometimes a repeated word is enough. In the example, “measurements” connects the observation sentence to the graphing sentence.

When you revise, underline the topic sentence. For each detail, ask what job it does. Can you point to a word or phrase that connects it to the main idea? If two sentences do the same job, keep the clearer one. If an important idea seems to appear suddenly, add the evidence or explanation a reader needs. Finally, read the paragraph aloud and listen for places where the connection is hard to follow.
