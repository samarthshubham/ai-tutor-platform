# How plants make food

A green plant builds sugar from carbon dioxide and water using energy from light. This process is called photosynthesis. The sugar contains chemical energy. A plant can use it in cellular respiration, join sugar molecules to make starch for storage, or use sugar as building material for new tissues. The minerals absorbed by roots are important nutrients, but they are not the source of most of the carbon in a leaf.

## Where the ingredients come from

Water enters through the roots and travels through tubes called xylem. Carbon dioxide from the air enters a leaf through tiny openings called stomata. These openings also let water vapour out. A plant that closes its stomata on a hot afternoon can reduce water loss, but less carbon dioxide enters at the same time.

Many leaf cells contain chloroplasts. Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll, a pigment that absorbs light. Chlorophyll absorbs much of the red and blue light that hits it. More green light is reflected or transmitted, which helps explain why many leaves look green.

## A simplified equation

One way to summarize photosynthesis is:

```text
6 carbon dioxide + 6 water + light energy → 1 glucose + 6 oxygen
```

The numbers describe the proportions in a balanced summary equation. On each side there are six carbon atoms, twelve hydrogen atoms, and eighteen oxygen atoms. Real cells carry out many separate reactions. Some reactions capture light energy and split water, releasing oxygen. Other reactions use captured energy to build small carbon-containing molecules from carbon dioxide. A plant can assemble these molecules into glucose.

## Worked example: counting molecules

Imagine the simplified reaction forming three glucose molecules. For each glucose the equation uses six carbon dioxide molecules and produces six oxygen molecules. Multiply each number by three: eighteen carbon dioxide molecules are used, and eighteen oxygen molecules are produced. Each glucose contains six carbon atoms, so the three glucose molecules together contain eighteen carbon atoms. Those carbon atoms came from the eighteen carbon dioxide molecules.

## Comparing two leaves

Suppose two similar seedlings have enough water and carbon dioxide, but one spends an afternoon in bright light while the other is shaded. If light is the factor limiting the shaded plant, you would expect the brightly lit plant to capture more energy and make more sugar over that period. To test the effect of light, keep the species, soil, watering and temperature alike. Measure the same outcome on both plants, such as the change in oxygen around similar amounts of leaf tissue. Plants also use oxygen in respiration, so an oxygen reading represents both processes acting together. Repeating your comparison with several plants will give a better picture than relying on a single pair.
